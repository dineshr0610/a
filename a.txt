============================================================ PART 1 —
SECURITY FUNDAMENTALS
============================================================

1.  What is information security? Information security means protecting
    information and information systems from unauthorized access, use,
    disclosure, modification, destruction, or disruption. Its purpose is
    to keep information private when necessary, accurate, and available
    to authorized users.

2.  What is the CIA triad? The CIA triad is the three basic goals of
    information security: • Confidentiality: only authorized people or
    systems can see the information. • Integrity: information remains
    accurate and is not changed without authorization. • Availability:
    authorized users can access information and services when needed.

Example: A hospital record must be private (confidentiality), correct
(integrity), and available to authorized doctors when needed
(availability).

3.  What is confidentiality? Confidentiality protects information from
    being disclosed to unauthorized people. Encryption, access control,
    strong authentication, and careful handling of keys help provide
    confidentiality. Example: encrypting a confidential government
    document before sending it.

4.  What is integrity? Integrity means data has not been improperly
    altered, deleted, or corrupted. Hashes and digital signatures can
    help detect changes. Access controls and audit logs also help
    protect integrity. Important: an ordinary hash can detect a change
    only if the expected hash is trusted. An attacker who can replace
    both a file and its stored hash may defeat a simple check.

5.  What is availability? Availability means authorized users can access
    a system or information when required. Backups, redundancy,
    maintenance, disaster recovery, and protection against
    denial-of-service attacks help availability.

6.  What is authentication? Authentication checks an identity: “Who are
    you?” Examples include passwords, one-time codes, security keys, and
    digital certificates.

7.  What is authorization? Authorization determines what an
    authenticated user is allowed to do. Authentication identifies the
    user; authorization decides their permissions.

8.  What is non-repudiation? Non-repudiation provides evidence that can
    help prevent a party from plausibly denying an action, such as
    signing a document. Digital signatures can support it, but the
    result also depends on private-key protection, identity
    verification, trustworthy records, and applicable procedures. A
    signature alone does not automatically prove who physically operated
    a key.

9.  What is cryptography? Cryptography uses mathematical techniques to
    protect information. It includes encryption, decryption, hashing,
    digital signatures, and key-establishment protocols.

10. What is plaintext, ciphertext, encryption, and decryption? •
    Plaintext: the original readable message. • Ciphertext: the
    transformed, usually unreadable message. • Encryption: transforming
    plaintext into ciphertext using an algorithm and key. • Decryption:
    recovering plaintext from ciphertext using the appropriate key.

11. What is a cryptographic key? A key is a value used by a
    cryptographic algorithm to encrypt, decrypt, sign, verify, or
    establish a shared secret. Security depends heavily on keeping
    secret keys secret and managing keys correctly.

12. What is the difference between a cipher and a hash? A cipher is used
    for encryption and usually has a corresponding decryption process. A
    cryptographic hash maps data of any practical length to a fixed-size
    digest and is designed to be one-way; it is not decrypted to recover
    the original message.

13. What is symmetric-key cryptography? Symmetric cryptography uses the
    same secret key, or a closely related shared secret, for encryption
    and decryption. Examples in these experiments are DES and AES.
    Advantages: fast and suitable for large amounts of data. Limitation:
    the parties need a secure way to share and protect the secret key.

14. What is asymmetric-key cryptography? Asymmetric cryptography uses a
    mathematically related key pair: a public key and a private key. The
    public key may be shared; the private key must be protected. RSA is
    used in the RSA encryption and digital-signature experiments.
    Diffie–Hellman is a public-key key-agreement method, not a
    message-encryption algorithm by itself.

15. What is the difference between symmetric and asymmetric
    cryptography? Symmetric cryptography is generally faster and uses a
    shared secret key. Asymmetric cryptography uses a public/private key
    pair and helps with tasks such as key establishment and signatures,
    but is generally slower. Real systems commonly combine both:
    asymmetric cryptography establishes or protects a symmetric key, and
    symmetric cryptography protects the bulk data.

16. What is a substitution cipher? A substitution cipher replaces a
    letter or group of letters with other letters according to a rule.
    Caesar and Playfair are examples. The letters’ positions may be
    replaced while their order is handled according to the substitution
    rule.

17. What is a transposition cipher? A transposition cipher rearranges
    the positions of plaintext characters without replacing the
    characters themselves. The row-major and column-major
    transformations in Experiment 2 are transposition methods.

18. What is brute force? A brute-force attack tries possible keys or
    passwords until a result is found. Caesar cipher has only 25 nonzero
    shifts, so trying every shift is easy. Strong cryptographic systems
    use sufficiently large key spaces and additional protections to make
    brute-force search impractical.

19. What is an attack? An attack is an attempt to compromise
    confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication, or another
    security goal. Examples include brute force, interception,
    tampering, replay, and denial of service.

============================================================ EXPERIMENT
1 — CAESAR CIPHER
============================================================

Objective Understand a substitution cipher and perform encryption and
decryption by shifting letters.

1.  What is Caesar cipher? Caesar cipher is a simple monoalphabetic
    substitution cipher. Each letter is shifted by a fixed number of
    alphabet positions. For example, with a shift of 3, A becomes D, B
    becomes E, and X wraps around to A.

2.  Why is it called a substitution cipher? Because each plaintext
    letter is replaced by another letter. In its basic form, the same
    shift is used for all letters.

3.  How does Caesar encryption work? Map letters to numbers from 0
    to 25. Add the shift key, then take the result modulo 26 so it wraps
    around the alphabet. Convert the resulting number back to a letter.

4.  How does decryption work? Subtract the same shift value instead of
    adding it. This reverses the encryption. If the shift is 3, decrypt
    by shifting backward 3 positions.

5.  Why is modulo 26 used? The English alphabet has 26 letters. Modulo
    26 keeps the result between 0 and 25 and makes wraparound possible.
    For example, shifting Z forward by 1 returns A.

6.  What is a key in Caesar cipher? The key is the shift value. There
    are 26 possible shifts if shift 0 is included, but shift 0 changes
    nothing, so there are only 25 meaningful nonzero shifts.

7.  Does Caesar cipher preserve uppercase and lowercase? A properly
    designed implementation can preserve case by treating uppercase and
    lowercase letters separately. Spaces, digits, and punctuation can be
    left unchanged, as required by the lab sheet.

8.  What are ASCII and ord/chr used for conceptually? ASCII assigns
    numeric codes to characters. A program can use character codes to
    calculate a letter’s alphabet position, shift it, and convert it
    back to a character. The important concept is character-to-number
    and number-to-character conversion.

9.  What is a Caesar brute-force attack? It tries every possible shift
    and displays each candidate plaintext. Since the key space is very
    small, an attacker can quickly identify the meaningful result.

10. What are the advantages of Caesar cipher? It is simple to
    understand, easy to perform manually, and useful for learning
    substitution and modular arithmetic.

11. What are its limitations? It has a tiny key space, preserves
    language patterns, and is easily broken by brute force or frequency
    analysis. It must not be used to protect real confidential data.

12. What is frequency analysis? It studies how often letters or groups
    of letters occur. In natural English, some letters such as E occur
    more frequently than others. Simple substitution ciphers preserve
    enough patterns for frequency analysis to help recover the message.

13. What is the difference between Caesar and modern encryption? Caesar
    uses a tiny shift key and simple letter substitution. Modern
    cryptographic algorithms use much stronger mathematical designs,
    large keys, and carefully designed modes and protocols.

14. What does “shift only vowels” or “shift only consonants” mean in the
    lab exercises? It is a variation of the basic Caesar rule. The
    program applies the shift only to the selected category of letters
    and leaves other characters unchanged. This is an exercise
    variation, not a secure cryptographic method.

15. Viva summary Caesar is a monoalphabetic substitution cipher;
    encryption shifts forward, decryption shifts backward, modulo 26
    handles wraparound, and brute force is easy because the key space is
    tiny.

1-b — PLAYFAIR CIPHER
============================================================

Objective Encrypt and decrypt pairs of letters using a 5 × 5 key matrix
generated from a keyword.

1.  What is Playfair cipher? Playfair is a classical digraph
    substitution cipher. Instead of encrypting one letter at a time, it
    encrypts two letters together using positions in a 5 × 5 matrix.

2.  Why is the matrix 5 × 5? The English alphabet has 26 letters, but a
    5 × 5 matrix has 25 cells. Playfair conventionally combines I and J
    into one cell, leaving 25 letters.

3.  How is the key matrix created? Write the keyword in uppercase,
    remove repeated letters, treat J as I, then append the remaining
    unused alphabet letters. Arrange the resulting 25 letters in a 5 × 5
    matrix.

4.  Why are duplicate keyword letters removed? Each letter must occupy
    only one cell. Removing duplicates creates a matrix with 25 distinct
    alphabet symbols under the I/J convention.

5.  What is plaintext preprocessing? Before encryption, convert letters
    to a consistent case, replace J with I, split the message into
    pairs, separate repeated letters in a pair by inserting X, and
    append X if a final unmatched letter remains.

6.  Why is X inserted between repeated letters? A Playfair pair should
    not contain the same letter twice. If a pair contains repeated
    letters, an X is inserted to separate them. For example, a repeated
    pair such as LL is split using X before forming the next pair.

7.  Why is X added at the end? The algorithm processes two letters at a
    time. If the cleaned plaintext has an odd number of letters, X
    supplies a partner for the final letter.

8.  What are the three Playfair encryption rules? • Same row: replace
    each letter with the letter immediately to its right, wrapping
    around at the row end. • Same column: replace each letter with the
    letter immediately below it, wrapping around at the bottom. •
    Rectangle: if the letters are in different rows and columns, take
    the letter in the same row as each original letter but in the other
    letter’s column.

9.  How does Playfair decryption work? For a pair in the same row, move
    left instead of right. For a pair in the same column, move up
    instead of down. For the rectangle rule, use the same column
    exchange rule. The I/J convention still applies.

10. Why are pairs used? Encrypting pairs makes Playfair more complex
    than a simple one-letter substitution because the transformation
    depends on the relationship between two letters’ positions.

11. What are the advantages? It is more difficult to break by simple
    single-letter frequency analysis than Caesar cipher because it
    encrypts pairs rather than individual letters. It is also useful for
    learning matrix-based classical encryption rules.

12. What are the limitations? It is a classical cipher and is not secure
    by modern standards. Digraph patterns and other statistical methods
    can help break it. It also needs preprocessing conventions, and
    decryption cannot always tell whether an X was originally in the
    message or was inserted as padding.

13. Why might decrypted text contain X? X may have been inserted between
    repeated letters or at the end to make pairs. The receiver may need
    to remove padding X characters by interpreting the message;
    automatic removal can accidentally delete genuine X letters.

14. What is a digraph? A digraph is a pair of characters processed
    together. Playfair operates on digraphs.

15. Viva summary Playfair uses a keyword-generated 5 × 5 matrix,
    combines I/J, preprocesses plaintext into pairs, and uses same-row,
    same-column, and rectangle rules.

1-b — HILL CIPHER
============================================================

Objective Use a 2 × 2 key matrix and modulo 26 matrix arithmetic to
encrypt and decrypt letter pairs.

1.  What is Hill cipher? Hill cipher is a polygraphic substitution
    cipher that encrypts groups of letters using linear algebra. In this
    experiment, two letters are represented as a vector and multiplied
    by a 2 × 2 key matrix modulo 26.

2.  Why is it called a polygraphic cipher? Because it transforms a group
    of letters together rather than treating each letter independently.

3.  How are letters converted to numbers? Use A = 0, B = 1, …, Z = 25.
    Each plaintext pair becomes a two-element numerical vector.

4.  How does encryption work? Multiply the key matrix by the plaintext
    vector, then reduce each resulting value modulo 26. Convert the two
    resulting numbers back into letters to obtain the ciphertext pair.

5.  Why is modulo 26 used? It maps the arithmetic results back to the
    26-letter alphabet.

6.  Why must the key matrix be invertible? Decryption requires an
    inverse matrix. If the key matrix has no inverse modulo 26, the
    plaintext cannot be uniquely recovered for every ciphertext pair.

7.  What is the determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix? For matrix [[a,b],[c,d]],
    the determinant is ad − bc.

8.  What makes a 2 × 2 Hill key valid modulo 26? The determinant must
    have a multiplicative inverse modulo 26. This happens exactly when
    gcd(determinant, 26) = 1. In other words, the determinant must be
    relatively prime to 26.

9.  What is a modular inverse? A number x is the modular inverse of a
    modulo 26 if a × x leaves remainder 1 when divided by 26. It is used
    to construct the inverse key matrix.

10. How does decryption work? Find the inverse key matrix modulo 26.
    Multiply it by the ciphertext vector and reduce each value
    modulo 26. The result gives the original plaintext letters.

11. Why is padding needed? The 2 × 2 matrix processes two letters at a
    time. If the plaintext has an odd number of letters, a padding
    character such as X is added to complete the final pair.

12. What are the advantages? Hill cipher illustrates matrix
    multiplication, modular arithmetic, determinants, and inverse
    matrices. It transforms letter groups and can obscure simple
    single-letter patterns.

13. What are the limitations? It is not secure against modern
    cryptanalysis. With enough known plaintext/ciphertext pairs, the key
    matrix can be recovered. The basic version also depends on correct
    key invertibility and padding.

14. What is the difference between Playfair and Hill? Playfair uses
    letter positions in a keyword matrix and applies
    row/column/rectangle rules. Hill maps letters to numbers and uses
    matrix multiplication modulo 26.

15. Viva summary Hill is a polygraphic cipher. Convert letters to
    numbers, multiply by a 2 × 2 key matrix modulo 26, and decrypt with
    the modular inverse matrix. The determinant must be relatively prime
    to 26.

2 — RAIL FENCE / ROW-MAJOR AND COLUMN-MAJOR TRANSPOSITION
============================================================

Objective Perform transposition encryption and decryption using a
matrix, a user-selected number of columns, and X padding when necessary.

1.  What is a transposition cipher? A transposition cipher rearranges
    the positions of characters. Unlike substitution, it does not
    replace each letter with a different letter; it changes their order.

2.  What does the experiment require? Accept plaintext and a number of
    columns, pad with X when needed, construct and display the matrix,
    perform row-major and column-major transformations, and recover the
    plaintext by reversing each transformation.

3.  What is row-major transformation in this lab? Fill the matrix column
    by column, then read it row by row to produce the ciphertext. For
    decryption, reconstruct the matrix from the ciphertext in row order
    and read it column by column.

4.  What is column-major transformation in this lab? Fill the matrix row
    by row, then read it column by column to produce the ciphertext. For
    decryption, reconstruct the matrix column by column and read it row
    by row.

5.  Why is the matrix needed? The matrix defines the arrangement of
    letters. The selected number of columns determines how many
    characters fit in each row and how many rows are needed.

6.  Why is X padding used? If the number of characters does not fill the
    final row or matrix evenly, X characters are added to complete the
    matrix. This makes the reading pattern consistent.

7.  Why must encryption and decryption use matching dimensions? The
    receiver needs to know the number of columns and the matrix
    dimensions to put the ciphertext characters back into their correct
    positions.

8.  Is this the same as modern Rail Fence cipher? The lab sheet labels
    the tasks as rail fence techniques, but its specific required
    operations are matrix-based row-major and column-major
    transformations. In viva, describe the exact transformations
    specified by the lab rather than confusing them with every variant
    of the classical zigzag Rail Fence cipher.

9.  What happens to the letters in transposition? The letters remain the
    same, but their positions change. The ciphertext contains the same
    letters as the padded plaintext, in a different order.

10. How is decryption performed? Reverse the read/fill operations. For
    row-major encryption, reconstruct by rows and read down columns. For
    column-major encryption, reconstruct by columns and read across
    rows.

11. What is the main limitation of a basic transposition cipher? It does
    not change letter frequencies; it only rearranges positions. It is a
    classical method and is not secure against modern analysis when used
    alone.

12. What is the difference between substitution and transposition?
    Substitution changes symbols according to a mapping. Transposition
    rearranges the positions of the original symbols.

13. Why may the recovered text contain X? The X may be padding inserted
    to fill the matrix. Removing trailing X characters can be ambiguous
    if the original message itself ended with X.

14. Viva summary Transposition changes character positions, not
    character identities. Row-major in this lab means fill down columns
    and read across rows; column-major means fill across rows and read
    down columns. X completes the matrix.

3 — DES (DATA ENCRYPTION STANDARD)
============================================================

Objective Implement DES encryption and decryption using PyCryptodome.

1.  What is DES? DES stands for Data Encryption Standard. It is a
    symmetric-key block cipher that processes data in 64-bit blocks. Its
    key is represented by 8 bytes, but only 56 bits are effective key
    bits; the remaining 8 bits are used for parity.

2.  What is a block cipher? A block cipher transforms fixed-size blocks
    of data using a key. DES has a 64-bit block size. Since a message
    may not fit a complete block, a mode and possibly padding are
    required.

3.  Is DES symmetric or asymmetric? DES is symmetric. The same secret
    key is used to encrypt and decrypt.

4.  What is DES key length? The key is supplied as 8 bytes (64 bits),
    but 8 of those bits are parity bits, so the effective cryptographic
    key length is 56 bits.

5.  How does DES work at a high level? DES uses a 16-round Feistel
    structure. It splits a 64-bit data block into left and right halves,
    applies expansion, key mixing, substitution, and permutation
    operations in rounds, then combines the halves to produce
    ciphertext. Decryption uses the same overall structure with the
    round keys in reverse order.

6.  What is a Feistel network? A design that splits a block into two
    halves and repeatedly transforms one half using a round function and
    key, combining it with the other half. One useful property is that
    encryption and decryption can use the same basic structure with
    reversed round-key order.

7.  What is a DES round? A round is one stage of the repeated
    transformation. DES has 16 rounds; each uses a round key derived
    from the main key.

8.  Why is padding needed in the lab program? The DES block size is 8
    bytes. If plaintext is not a multiple of 8 bytes, padding such as
    PKCS#7 is added so the final data can be processed as complete
    blocks. Padding is removed after decryption.

9.  What is ECB mode? Electronic Codebook (ECB) encrypts each block
    independently with the same key. Identical plaintext blocks produce
    identical ciphertext blocks. This reveals patterns, so ECB is
    unsuitable for real-world confidential data even when the underlying
    cipher is strong.

10. Why is DES no longer considered secure? Its 56-bit effective key is
    too small against modern brute-force capabilities. DES has been
    replaced by stronger approaches, especially AES.

11. What is ciphertext in hexadecimal? Ciphertext is binary data.
    Hexadecimal is a convenient text representation in which each byte
    is commonly represented by two hex characters. Hex output is a
    representation, not an extra layer of encryption.

12. What are the advantages and disadvantages of DES? Advantage:
    historically important and useful for understanding block ciphers
    and Feistel networks. Disadvantage: the key is too short for modern
    security and the 64-bit block size is small by current standards.

13. What is the difference between DES and AES? DES has a 64-bit block
    and 56 effective key bits, with 16 Feistel rounds. AES has a 128-bit
    block and 128-, 192-, or 256-bit keys, and uses a
    substitution-permutation network. AES is the modern standard for
    general symmetric encryption.

14. Viva summary DES is a symmetric 64-bit block cipher with an 8-byte
    key representation and 56 effective key bits. It uses 16 Feistel
    rounds. It is now insecure because the effective key is too short.

4 — AES (ADVANCED ENCRYPTION STANDARD)
============================================================

Objective Use AES-128 to encrypt and decrypt plaintext with a 16-byte
key, as required by the lab sheet.

1.  What is AES? AES stands for Advanced Encryption Standard. It is a
    widely used symmetric-key block cipher standardized by NIST. It is
    used for protecting data in many modern systems.

2.  Is AES symmetric or asymmetric? AES is symmetric. The same secret
    key is used for encryption and decryption.

3.  What is AES block size? AES always processes 128-bit (16-byte)
    blocks, regardless of key size.

4.  What are the AES key sizes? AES supports 128-bit, 192-bit, and
    256-bit keys, corresponding to 16, 24, and 32 bytes. The lab
    specifically requires AES-128, which uses a 16-byte key.

5.  What is the difference between AES block size and key size? Block
    size is the amount of data processed in one block: 128 bits. Key
    size is the size of the secret key: 128, 192, or 256 bits.

6.  How does AES work at a high level? AES arranges a 128-bit block into
    a 4 × 4 byte state and applies repeated rounds of transformations.
    These include SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns (except in the final
    round), and AddRoundKey. The number of rounds depends on key size:
    10 for AES-128, 12 for AES-192, and 14 for AES-256.

7.  What is SubBytes? A nonlinear substitution step that replaces each
    state byte using a fixed substitution table called the S-box.

8.  What is ShiftRows? A step that cyclically shifts rows of the AES
    state by different offsets.

9.  What is MixColumns? A step that combines the bytes within each
    column using arithmetic in a finite field. It spreads the influence
    of input bytes through the state.

10. What is AddRoundKey? A step that combines the state with a round key
    derived from the original key, using XOR.

11. What is ECB mode in this experiment? ECB encrypts each block
    independently. The lab sheet explicitly specifies ECB mode. However,
    ECB leaks patterns when equal plaintext blocks occur, so it is not
    recommended for real confidential data. Secure real applications
    commonly use authenticated modes such as AES-GCM, with correct nonce
    handling.

12. Why is padding required? AES processes blocks of 16 bytes. If the
    plaintext length is not a multiple of 16 bytes, a padding scheme
    such as PKCS#7 can extend it to a complete block. After decryption,
    the padding must be removed.

13. Why is the key required to be exactly 16 bytes in the lab? Because
    the lab asks for AES-128, which uses a 128-bit (16-byte) key. The
    key’s encoded byte length matters, not just the number of visible
    characters if non-ASCII characters are used.

14. Why is AES preferred over DES? AES supports much stronger key sizes
    and a 128-bit block size, and is efficient in software and hardware.
    DES’s 56-bit effective key is no longer sufficient.

15. Where is AES used? It is used in file and disk encryption, secure
    communications, wireless security, cloud services, and many
    protocols and applications.

16. Viva summary AES is a symmetric block cipher with a 128-bit block
    and key sizes of 128, 192, or 256 bits. The lab uses AES-128, ECB
    mode, and padding. ECB is a lab requirement, not a recommended
    real-world mode.

5 — RSA ENCRYPTION
============================================================

Objective Generate a 2048-bit RSA key pair, encrypt plaintext with the
public key using OAEP, and decrypt with the private key.

1.  What is RSA? RSA is an asymmetric cryptographic algorithm named
    after Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman. It uses a public/private key
    pair. In the lab, the public key encrypts and the matching private
    key decrypts.

2.  What are the public and private keys? The public key may be shared
    with others. The private key must remain secret. A sender can use
    the recipient’s public key to encrypt data intended for that
    recipient; the recipient uses their private key to decrypt it.

3.  What is the basic mathematical idea behind RSA? RSA is based on
    properties of modular arithmetic and the difficulty of factoring a
    large number formed as the product of two large primes. Real
    implementations use carefully generated keys and secure padding
    schemes.

4.  What is a 2048-bit RSA key? It is an RSA modulus of 2048 bits. The
    lab requires this key size. Key length alone does not make an
    implementation secure; correct key generation, padding, and key
    management also matter.

5.  What is OAEP? OAEP stands for Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding.
    It is a randomized padding scheme used with RSA encryption to
    provide stronger security than textbook RSA. The lab specifically
    requires RSA-OAEP.

6.  Why should textbook RSA not be used directly? Raw textbook RSA is
    deterministic and has mathematical weaknesses when used without a
    secure padding scheme. OAEP is designed to address important
    problems in RSA encryption.

7.  How does RSA encryption work in this experiment? Generate the key
    pair, encode the message as bytes, encrypt using the public key and
    OAEP, display ciphertext as hexadecimal, decrypt using the private
    key and OAEP, and compare the recovered message with the original.

8.  Can RSA encrypt a message of any length? No. RSA-OAEP has a maximum
    message size determined by the RSA modulus size and the hash used by
    OAEP. For a 2048-bit key with SHA-1 OAEP, the maximum is 214 bytes;
    with SHA-256 OAEP, it is 190 bytes. Large messages are usually
    encrypted with a symmetric algorithm such as AES, and RSA-OAEP
    protects the AES key.

9.  Is RSA faster than AES? No. RSA operations are generally much slower
    than symmetric encryption. RSA is used for tasks such as key
    transport or signatures, while AES is suited to encrypting large
    data.

10. Is RSA encryption the same as RSA digital signature? No. For
    confidentiality, encrypt with the recipient’s public key and decrypt
    with their private key. For a digital signature, sign with the
    sender’s private key and verify with the sender’s public key. These
    operations have different goals and secure schemes.

11. What is ciphertext in hexadecimal? It is the encrypted byte sequence
    represented with hexadecimal digits for display or copying. It is
    not plaintext and does not make the ciphertext more secure by
    itself.

12. What are RSA applications? Public-key encryption, digital
    signatures, certificates, and protecting or transporting symmetric
    keys. In modern systems, RSA is often used with symmetric encryption
    rather than to encrypt whole files directly.

13. What are RSA limitations? It is slower than symmetric encryption,
    requires secure key generation and storage, and must use appropriate
    padding. The private key must be protected.

14. Viva summary RSA is asymmetric. The lab generates a 2048-bit key
    pair, encrypts with the public key using OAEP, and decrypts with the
    private key. RSA is not suitable for directly encrypting
    unlimited-length messages.

6 — DIFFIE–HELLMAN KEY EXCHANGE
============================================================

Objective Show how Alice and Bob can independently calculate a shared
secret over an insecure network without sending the secret itself.

1.  What is Diffie–Hellman? Diffie–Hellman is a key-agreement protocol.
    It lets two parties establish a common secret over a channel that an
    eavesdropper may observe. It does not directly encrypt or decrypt
    messages.

2.  What public parameters do the parties agree on? The experiment asks
    for a prime number p and a primitive root g modulo p. These are
    public parameters and may be known to everyone.

3.  What is a private key in Diffie–Hellman? Each participant chooses a
    secret private exponent. Alice has a and Bob has b. The private
    exponents are never sent across the network.

4.  How is a public key calculated? Alice computes A = g^a mod p. Bob
    computes B = g^b mod p. These public values are exchanged.

5.  How is the shared secret calculated? Alice computes B^a mod p. Bob
    computes A^b mod p. Both calculations equal g^(ab) mod p, so the
    parties obtain the same shared value.

6.  Why do both sides get the same secret? Because modular
    exponentiation gives: (B^a mod p) = (g^(b))a mod p = g^(ab) mod p,
    and (A^b mod p) = (g^(a))b mod p = g^(ab) mod p. The exponents are
    multiplied in either order, so the results match.

7.  What is a primitive root? For a prime modulus p, a primitive root
    modulo p is a number whose powers generate all nonzero residues
    modulo p. The experiment uses it as the common base g.

8.  What is modular exponentiation? It calculates powers while
    repeatedly reducing modulo p. It allows large powers to be computed
    efficiently without constructing enormous integers unnecessarily.

9.  Does Diffie–Hellman transmit the secret key? No. Each side transmits
    only its public value. The secret is computed independently by both
    sides.

10. Does basic Diffie–Hellman authenticate Alice and Bob? No. Without
    authentication, a man-in-the-middle attacker can establish separate
    secrets with Alice and Bob. In real protocols, Diffie–Hellman is
    combined with authentication, such as certificates and digital
    signatures.

11. What is a man-in-the-middle attack? An attacker positions themselves
    between two parties and relays or alters communication. In
    unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman, the attacker can replace public
    values and establish different secrets with each party, potentially
    reading and modifying messages.

12. What happens after the shared secret is established? A secure
    protocol normally derives cryptographic session keys from the shared
    secret using a key derivation function. The resulting symmetric keys
    can then be used with an algorithm such as AES.

13. What is the difference between Diffie–Hellman and RSA?
    Diffie–Hellman is primarily a key-agreement mechanism: both parties
    contribute to a shared secret. RSA in the lab is used for public-key
    encryption and decryption. RSA can also be used for signatures, but
    that is a different operation.

14. Why are the lab values small? Small values such as p = 23 make
    calculations easy to check by hand. They are only for demonstration
    and are not secure for real communication.

15. What is the multiple-user extension? Each participant generates a
    public value from their private exponent. Every pair can use the
    other participant’s public value with their own private exponent to
    calculate a pairwise shared secret. Each pair should obtain matching
    values.

16. Viva summary Diffie–Hellman allows two parties to agree on a shared
    secret without transmitting it. It uses public p and g, private
    exponents, exchanged public values, and modular exponentiation.
    Authentication is needed to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks.

============================================================ EXPERIMENT
7 — SHA-1 MESSAGE DIGEST
============================================================

Objective Generate a SHA-1 digest, demonstrate the avalanche effect,
compare messages, and illustrate SHA-1 preprocessing.

1.  What is a cryptographic hash function? A hash function takes input
    data of arbitrary length and produces a fixed-length digest. A
    cryptographic hash is designed to be one-way and to have properties
    such as preimage resistance and collision resistance.

2.  What is SHA-1? SHA-1 stands for Secure Hash Algorithm 1. It produces
    a 160-bit (20-byte) digest, usually displayed as 40 hexadecimal
    characters.

3.  Is SHA-1 encryption? No. Hashing is not encryption. There is no
    decryption key to recover the original message from a SHA-1 digest.
    The digest is a one-way fingerprint of the input.

4.  What is a message digest? A message digest is the fixed-length
    output of a hash function. It is used as a compact fingerprint of
    data.

5.  What is the avalanche effect? A small change in the input should
    cause a large, unpredictable-looking change in the output digest.
    Changing one character should not merely change one corresponding
    character in the hash.

6.  What is SHA-1 used for historically? It has been used for integrity
    checks and older digital-signature systems. However, SHA-1 has
    practical collision weaknesses and is not recommended for
    security-sensitive new applications.

7.  What is a collision? A collision occurs when two different inputs
    produce the same hash digest. Since the input space is much larger
    than the fixed output space, collisions must exist mathematically;
    secure hash functions aim to make finding one computationally
    infeasible.

8.  What is preimage resistance? Given a digest, it should be
    computationally infeasible to find an input that hashes to that
    digest.

9.  What is second-preimage resistance? Given one specific input, it
    should be computationally infeasible to find a different input with
    the same digest.

10. What is SHA-1 preprocessing? The input is encoded as bits. A single
    1 bit is appended, then enough 0 bits are added so the length is 448
    modulo 512. Finally, a 64-bit representation of the original message
    length is appended. The padded message length becomes a multiple of
    512 bits.

11. Why are 512-bit blocks used? SHA-1 processes the padded message in
    512-bit blocks. Each block is processed through the hash algorithm’s
    internal rounds and state updates.

12. Why append the original length? The length field is part of the
    specified padding format and helps distinguish message lengths in
    the final block structure.

13. Does displaying preprocessing implement all of SHA-1? No. Showing
    the binary message, padding, and 512-bit blocks illustrates the
    preprocessing stage. A library such as hashlib performs the complete
    SHA-1 hashing algorithm, including the compression rounds.

14. Can SHA-1 be used for secure digital signatures today? It should not
    be used for new security-sensitive digital signatures because
    collision attacks undermine its security. SHA-256 or a stronger
    modern hash is preferred.

15. What is the difference between SHA-1 and SHA-256? SHA-1 produces a
    160-bit digest (40 hex characters) and is considered broken for
    collision resistance. SHA-256 produces a 256-bit digest (64 hex
    characters) and is widely used for modern integrity and signature
    applications.

16. Viva summary SHA-1 is a one-way hash that produces 160 bits. It is
    not encryption. Its lab exercises demonstrate digest generation,
    avalanche effect, comparison, and 512-bit-block preprocessing. SHA-1
    is no longer recommended for security-sensitive use.

============================================================ EXPERIMENT
8 — MD5 AND FILE INTEGRITY MONITORING
============================================================

Objective Generate MD5 digests, compare hashes, check file integrity,
and maintain a JSON record of file checksums.

1.  What is MD5? MD5 stands for Message Digest Algorithm 5. It maps
    input data of arbitrary length to a 128-bit (16-byte) digest,
    commonly displayed as 32 hexadecimal characters.

2.  Is MD5 encryption? No. MD5 is a hash function. It is not reversible
    encryption and does not use a decryption key.

3.  What is an MD5 checksum? It is the MD5 digest of a message or file.
    It can act as a fingerprint for detecting changes, especially
    accidental changes, if the expected checksum is trusted.

4.  What are the main menu functions in the lab? Generate an MD5 digest;
    verify message integrity against a supplied digest; demonstrate
    avalanche effect using two similar strings; calculate an MD5
    checksum for a text file; compare MD5, SHA-1, and SHA-256; exit.

5.  Why read a file in binary mode when calculating a checksum? A hash
    is calculated over bytes. Binary mode reads the file’s bytes
    directly without text decoding or newline conversion, which can
    otherwise change the byte sequence and checksum.

6.  What is the avalanche effect in MD5? A small input change usually
    produces a substantially different digest. This illustrates hash
    behavior, but it does not mean MD5 is secure against collisions.

7.  Why is MD5 no longer secure for cryptographic use? Practical
    collision attacks have been demonstrated. Attackers can construct
    different inputs with the same MD5 digest, so MD5 should not be used
    for digital signatures or other security-sensitive
    collision-resistant applications.

8.  Is MD5 still useful? It may still be encountered for legacy
    checksums or detecting accidental file changes where an attacker is
    not in a position to manipulate both the file and checksum. For
    adversarial integrity protection, use a modern cryptographic hash in
    an appropriate authenticated design.

9.  What is file integrity monitoring (FIM)? FIM tracks files and checks
    whether their contents have changed over time. A checksum is
    calculated for each file and compared with a previously stored
    checksum.

10. What is the purpose of the JSON record? The JSON file stores a
    mapping from each filename to its previously recorded checksum. The
    next run calculates current checksums and compares them with the
    stored record.

11. Explain the four FIM statuses. • Not Modified: the file exists and
    its current checksum matches the saved checksum. • Modified: the
    file exists, but its checksum differs from the saved checksum. • New
    File: a file exists now but was not in the saved record. • Missing
    File: a filename exists in the saved record but the file is no
    longer present.

12. Why update the JSON record after checking? The record is updated to
    represent the current observed state for comparison during a later
    run. Operationally, the baseline should be updated only according to
    a trusted policy; automatically accepting every change can hide
    malicious modifications.

13. What is the main limitation of checksum-based FIM? If an attacker
    can change both the file and the stored checksum record, the system
    may not detect the change. The record should be protected,
    access-controlled, and ideally stored or logged separately from the
    monitored files.

14. What is the difference between hashing a text and hashing a file?
    For text, the text is encoded into bytes before hashing. For a file,
    the hash is calculated over the file’s byte content. A large file
    can be read in chunks while maintaining the same overall hash result
    as reading all bytes at once.

15. Compare MD5, SHA-1, and SHA-256. • MD5: 128-bit digest, 32
    hexadecimal characters; collision-broken. • SHA-1: 160-bit digest,
    40 hexadecimal characters; collision-broken for secure use. •
    SHA-256: 256-bit digest, 64 hexadecimal characters; widely used for
    modern integrity and signatures. Digest length alone is not the only
    measure of security, but these lengths and known weaknesses are
    important distinctions.

16. Is a hash enough to authenticate a file? No. A plain hash provides
    no identity authentication if an attacker can replace both the file
    and its expected hash. A keyed MAC, digital signature, or trusted
    protected checksum record is needed when an active attacker is in
    scope.

17. Viva summary MD5 creates a 128-bit digest. It is a hash, not
    encryption, and it is no longer secure against collisions. The FIM
    extension stores file checksums in JSON and reports unchanged,
    modified, new, and missing files.

============================================================ EXPERIMENT
9 — DIGITAL SIGNATURE SYSTEM
============================================================

Objective Use an RSA key pair and SHA-256 to sign a message, verify the
signature, demonstrate tampering, and simulate email between Alice and
Bob.

1.  What is a digital signature? A digital signature is a cryptographic
    value created for a message using the sender’s private key and
    verified using the corresponding public key. It helps detect message
    changes and supports verification that the signature corresponds to
    the sender’s key.

2.  What are the three security goals associated with digital
    signatures? • Integrity: the signed content has not changed since it
    was signed. • Authentication: the signature verifies under the
    expected public key, provided the key is correctly associated with
    the sender. • Non-repudiation support: the signature can provide
    evidence that the corresponding private key signed the content. This
    depends on key protection, identity binding, and reliable records.

3.  How does signing work in this experiment? The message is hashed
    using SHA-256. The signing algorithm uses the sender’s private key
    to create a signature for that digest. The sender transmits the
    message and signature.

4.  How does verification work? The receiver calculates the SHA-256
    digest of the received message and checks the signature using the
    sender’s public key. If verification succeeds, the signature is
    valid for that message under that public key. If the message or
    signature changes, verification normally fails.

5.  Why hash before signing? Hashing produces a fixed-length digest of
    the message, making signature operations efficient and ensuring that
    the signature is tied to the message content. Standard signature
    schemes are designed to sign a digest safely.

6.  What is SHA-256? SHA-256 is a member of the SHA-2 family. It
    produces a 256-bit digest, usually represented by 64 hexadecimal
    characters. It is widely used in digital signatures and integrity
    checks.

7.  What is RSA-2048? It is RSA using a 2048-bit modulus. The lab
    requires at least a 2048-bit RSA key pair. The private key must be
    kept secret, while the public key is distributed to receivers.

8.  What is the difference between a signature and encryption?
    Encryption primarily provides confidentiality: it prevents
    unauthorized parties from reading data. A digital signature
    primarily supports integrity and authenticity. A signature does not
    hide the message; anyone who receives an unencrypted message can
    read it.

9.  Which key is used to sign and which key verifies? The sender signs
    using their private key. The receiver verifies using the
    corresponding public key. This is the reverse of the key roles used
    for RSA encryption to a recipient, where the recipient’s public key
    encrypts and their private key decrypts.

10. What is message tampering? Tampering is an unauthorized modification
    of the message. If a signed message is modified, the digest of the
    modified message differs, so verification against the original
    signature fails.

11. Why must the receiver trust the public key’s identity? A valid
    signature proves that the signature matches a particular key, not
    automatically that the key belongs to the claimed person. The
    receiver needs a trustworthy method—such as a verified certificate
    or trusted key distribution—to associate the public key with the
    sender.

12. What is a digital certificate? A digital certificate binds an
    identity or subject to a public key and is digitally signed by a
    certificate authority or another trusted issuer. It helps a receiver
    assess whether a public key belongs to the claimed party.

13. What is the role of the private key file? It stores secret signing
    material for later use. It must be protected against unauthorized
    reading or modification. The public key can be distributed, but it
    should still be obtained from a trusted source.

14. What happens in the Alice and Bob simulation? Alice generates a key
    pair, hashes and signs a message with her private key, then sends
    the message and signature. Bob verifies using Alice’s public key.
    When the received message is modified, verification fails because
    the signature no longer matches the modified message.

15. Does a digital signature guarantee confidentiality? No. The message
    may remain readable. To provide both confidentiality and
    authenticity, systems combine encryption with signatures or use an
    authenticated secure communication protocol.

16. What is the difference between RSA encryption and RSA signature? RSA
    encryption with OAEP is intended to protect confidentiality and uses
    the recipient’s public key for encryption. RSA signatures use a
    signature scheme and the sender’s private key for signing, then the
    sender’s public key for verification. Do not treat these as
    interchangeable operations.

17. What is the difference between a valid signature and a trusted
    sender? A valid signature means the signature verifies under a given
    public key for the supplied message. A trusted sender conclusion
    also requires confidence that the public key really belongs to the
    claimed sender and that the private key was controlled
    appropriately.

18. What is non-repudiation’s limitation? A signature is strong
    technical evidence, but it cannot alone prove every real-world fact
    about who used a key or under what authority. Private-key
    compromise, identity verification, timestamps, audit trails, and
    legal procedures can matter.

19. Viva summary The lab uses RSA-2048 and SHA-256. The sender hashes
    and signs with the private key; the receiver verifies with the
    public key. A modified message causes verification failure.
    Signatures support integrity, authentication, and non-repudiation,
    but do not encrypt the message.

============================================================ IMPORTANT
COMPARISONS FOR VIVA
============================================================

1.  Encryption vs hashing Encryption is reversible with the appropriate
    key and aims primarily at confidentiality. Hashing produces a
    fixed-length one-way digest and is commonly used for integrity
    checks and as part of signatures.

2.  Hashing vs digital signature A hash alone is not proof of who
    created a message. A digital signature binds a message digest to a
    private key and can be verified with the corresponding public key.

3.  DES vs AES DES: symmetric; 64-bit block; 56 effective key bits; 16
    Feistel rounds; obsolete for secure use. AES: symmetric; 128-bit
    block; 128-, 192-, or 256-bit key; modern widely used standard.

4.  RSA vs Diffie–Hellman RSA encryption uses a public key to encrypt
    and a private key to decrypt. Diffie–Hellman allows two parties to
    agree on a shared secret; it does not directly encrypt messages and
    needs authentication to resist man-in-the-middle attacks.

5.  Caesar vs Playfair vs Hill Caesar shifts individual letters.
    Playfair transforms letter pairs using a 5 × 5 keyword matrix. Hill
    transforms letter pairs using matrix multiplication modulo 26 and an
    invertible key matrix.

6.  Substitution vs transposition Substitution replaces symbols.
    Transposition changes their positions.

7.  MD5 vs SHA-1 vs SHA-256 MD5 outputs 128 bits; SHA-1 outputs 160
    bits; SHA-256 outputs 256 bits. MD5 and SHA-1 have known collision
    weaknesses and are not recommended for security-sensitive new
    applications. SHA-256 is widely used.

8.  Confidentiality vs integrity Confidentiality asks “Who can read it?”
    Integrity asks “Has it been changed improperly?” Encryption
    primarily supports confidentiality; hashes, MACs, and signatures can
    support integrity in different threat models.

9.  Authentication vs non-repudiation Authentication checks identity or
    key ownership in a particular context. Non-repudiation is about
    providing evidence of an action that may help prevent later denial.
    Digital signatures can support both, but trusted identity binding
    and key management matter.

10. Public key vs private key A public key may be shared and is used for
    operations such as verifying signatures or encrypting to its owner.
    A private key is secret and is used for signing or decrypting,
    depending on the algorithm and operation.

============================================================ SHORT
RAPID-FIRE VIVA QUESTIONS

Q: What does CIA stand for? A: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability.

Q: Which experiments use symmetric encryption? A: DES and AES.

Q: Which experiment demonstrates asymmetric encryption? A: RSA
encryption.

Q: Which experiment establishes a shared secret without sending it
directly? A: Diffie–Hellman key exchange.

Q: Which experiments use hashing? A: SHA-1, MD5, and digital signatures
using SHA-256; hashes can also be used in file integrity monitoring.

Q: Is a hash reversible? A: No; cryptographic hashing is designed to be
one-way.

Q: What is the SHA-1 digest length? A: 160 bits, usually 40 hexadecimal
characters.

Q: What is the MD5 digest length? A: 128 bits, usually 32 hexadecimal
characters.

Q: What is the SHA-256 digest length? A: 256 bits, usually 64
hexadecimal characters.

Q: What is the DES effective key length? A: 56 bits, represented as an
8-byte key with 8 parity bits.

Q: What is the AES block size? A: 128 bits.

Q: What AES key sizes are supported? A: 128, 192, and 256 bits.

Q: What key size does the RSA experiment require? A: 2048 bits.

Q: What is OAEP? A: Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding, used with RSA
encryption.

Q: Which key signs a digital message? A: The sender’s private key.

Q: Which key verifies a digital signature? A: The corresponding public
key.

Q: Does a digital signature encrypt the message? A: No.

Q: What does a collision mean? A: Two different inputs produce the same
hash digest.

Q: What is the avalanche effect? A: A small input change produces a
large change in the hash output.

Q: What does a file integrity monitor report? A: Not Modified, Modified,
New File, and Missing File.

Q: Why use binary mode for a file checksum? A: To hash the exact file
bytes without text decoding or newline conversion.

Q: Why is Caesar cipher insecure? A: It has only 25 meaningful nonzero
shifts and is easily brute-forced.

Q: Why must the Hill matrix be invertible modulo 26? A: Decryption
requires its modular inverse.

Q: Why does Playfair combine I and J? A: To fit the alphabet into a
25-cell matrix.

Q: Why does the transposition experiment add X? A: To fill the matrix
completely when the text length does not fit.

Q: What is a man-in-the-middle attack? A: An attacker intercepts and may
alter communication between two parties; unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman
is vulnerable to it.

Q: What is the safest general answer when asked whether a classical
cipher is secure today? A: No. Caesar, Playfair, Hill, and simple
transposition are educational classical ciphers, not modern secure
encryption.
